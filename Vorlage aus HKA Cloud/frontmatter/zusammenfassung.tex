As part of this project, an eye tracker was implemented in a driving simulator at Karlsruhe University of Applied Sciences using simple hardware. The aim was to record the gaze position of test subjects in various traffic scenarios in order to better analyse their perception and decision-making processes in road traffic. The aim of the project was not to develop a tracking system from scratch. Instead, existing eye tracking systems were to be evaluated for their applicability in the university's driving simulator and implemented. The main part of the project was to find a suitable eye tracking system, implement it in the CARLA environment and find solutions to any technical problems that might arise. 

After a market analysis, the Beam Eye Tracker from Eyeware Tech was selected due to its simple API, low purchase price, low hardware requirements and sufficient accuracy. The implementation was carried out using Python with the Beam SDK. The tracking data was linked to CARLA, in particular to the data from the instance segmentation sensor, in order to infer the objects viewed in the simulation from the gaze position estimated by the eye tracker. This allows the eye tracker not only to output the test subject's gaze position in x and y coordinates relative to the screen, but also to recognise which object the test subject is looking at within the CARLA simulation environment at any given time. The interface between the eye tracker data and the CARLA Instance Segmentation Sensor data was tested within a specially created test environment in CARLA. 

A key technical problem was the curved screen of the driving simulator, which led to distortions in the gaze data. To compensate for this, a software-based correction was implemented that uses mathematical functions to minimise the error caused by the curved screen as much as possible. The accuracy of the implemented tracking system delivered satisfactory results, especially considering the low financial outlay. It was possible to implement an eye tracking system completely free of charge. The only costs incurred were those of the webcam used. Implementation in the driving simulator and in the CARLA environment was successful.

The work proves that even with inexpensive hardware and software, it is possible to effectively integrate eye tracking systems into driving simulators with large, curved screens, thus laying the foundation for future experiments in driving behaviour analysis. The work also provides some approaches for further improving tracking accuracy in the future.
